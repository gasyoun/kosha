\begin{abstract}
Large language models are increasingly evaluated on lexicographic tasks---generating
dictionary-style definitions and selecting word senses---but Sanskrit, despite its
exceptionally rich lexicographic tradition, has been largely absent from this line of
work. The one published Sanskrit WSD-from-definitions system operates on the corpus side
of the Digital Corpus of Sanskrit (DCS). We present the first evaluation of this task
class on the \emph{dictionary} side: definition generation and gloss-grounded sense
selection scored against Monier-Williams and Heritage (Huet) glosses, connected to corpus
attestations through the DCS--CDSL headword crosswalk. All data are local, committed and
license-checked. Our definition-generation track evaluates five system arms on a frozen,
stratified 500-headword sample with deterministic metrics and a blinded LLM judge under
explicit gates; a cross-lingual re-scoring against Huet's French glosses shows the system
ranking to be reference-invariant. Our WSD track contributes a fully deterministic
gold---DCS word-sense annotations resolved onto MW sense divisions---and shows that a
gloss-grounded LLM arm (54.3\%) falls far short of a corpus-frequency baseline (84.3\%)
on held-out polysemous items, a negative result that locates the difficulty of Sanskrit
WSD in sense granularity and usage priors rather than in gloss access. Every number in
this paper reproduces from committed artifacts with one command per track.

\section{Introduction}

Computational lexicography for Sanskrit sits on two large, independently curated
resources: the dictionary tradition---above all Monier-Williams
\cite{monier-williams-1899} and Huet's Sanskrit Heritage Dictionary
\cite{huet-2026-heritage}---and the Digital Corpus of Sanskrit (DCS)
\cite{hellwig-dcs}, a 5.7-million-token morphologically annotated corpus whose recent
releases carry human word-sense annotations over 531{,}747 tokens. A growing
``definition modeling'' line of work evaluates language models on generating and using
dictionary definitions \cite{periti-etal-2025-definition,liu-etal-2026-lm}, and Hellwig
et al.\ \cite{hellwig-etal-2026-sanskrit} have shown that lexicographic definitions can
drive word sense disambiguation for Sanskrit at scale.

That system, however, lives entirely on the corpus side of the DCS--CDSL headword
crosswalk. The dictionary side---where the glosses are the object of evaluation rather
than a training signal---remains unevaluated: no definition-generation or
gloss-grounded WSD evaluation has been run over the CDSL dictionaries, and none over the
crosswalk that links them to corpus evidence. This paper closes that gap with two tracks
over one frozen headword universe.

\textbf{Track 1: definition generation.} Five system arms---a derangement floor, three
API baselines of two model families, and a strong-model reference---generate English
glosses for a frozen, frequency- and polysemy-stratified sample of 500 Monier-Williams
headwords, scored with chrF, token-overlap F1 and a blinded LLM judge under explicit
gates. A second reference re-scores the same arms against Huet's French glosses on the
333-headword MW$\cap$Heritage subset.

\textbf{Track 2: gloss-grounded WSD with a deterministic gold.} For polysemous lemmas of
the same universe we build a sense-selection task whose gold requires no manual
annotation: a DCS sentence, the lemma's numbered MW sense glosses, and the correct sense
recovered by joining DCS's own word-sense annotations to MW sense divisions through the
crosswalk. We score a gloss-grounded LLM arm against the corpus-frequency (MFS) baseline
that the same data support, with paired significance.

Our contributions are: (i) the first definition-generation evaluation on Sanskrit
lexicographic data, with its cross-lingual reference-invariance check; (ii) the first
gold-scored gloss-grounded WSD evaluation on the dictionary side of the crosswalk, built
without any new annotation; (iii) an honest negative result---prompting with glosses and
context is not competitive with corpus priors on this gold---and the error analysis that
localises it; and (iv) a fully reproducible evaluation stack (frozen samples, committed
artifacts, one-command re-scoring and validation).

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Definition modeling.} Periti et al.\ \cite{periti-etal-2025-definition}
survey definition generation for word meaning modeling across monolingual, multilingual
and cross-lingual settings; LM-Lexicon \cite{liu-etal-2026-lm} improves definition
modeling by harmonising semantic experts. Neither line touches Sanskrit. Our Track 1
imports their evaluation class (reference-based surface metrics plus a judged adequacy
score) into Sanskrit lexicography.

\paragraph{Sanskrit WSD.} Hellwig et al.\ \cite{hellwig-etal-2026-sanskrit} train a
supervised bi-encoder on MW lexicographic definitions and evaluate on DCS-anchored gold,
establishing that definitions are a usable sense signal for Sanskrit. Patel and Kulkarni
\cite{patel-kulkarni-2024-word} align senses \emph{across} Sanskrit lexica---the
operation our gold construction depends on, here used deterministically via the
DCS--CDSL crosswalk rather than as a learned model. Our Track 2 differs from
\cite{hellwig-etal-2026-sanskrit} in three deliberate ways: it is zero-shot prompting
rather than a fine-tuned system, it scores on the dictionary side of the crosswalk, and
it reports against a corpus-frequency baseline with paired significance, accepting in
advance that a negative outcome is a publishable finding.

\paragraph{LLM judges.} We use an LLM judge only under the standard gates---floor
separation and correlation with a deterministic metric---motivated by large-scale
evidence that LLM judgments do not automatically match human ones
\cite{bavaresco-etal-2025-llms}. A human-scored subsample is scheduled before
camera-ready and flagged in Limitations.

\section{Data}

All datasets are pre-existing, committed assets consumed, not rebuilt.

\textbf{A3: MW glosses.} 187{,}506 SLP1-keyed English glosses extracted from Monier-
Williams \cite{monier-williams-1899} (public domain, 1899). Glosses carry markup-
stripping artifacts; all arms share them equally.

\textbf{D20: Heritage glosses.} 24{,}549 entries from Huet's Sanskrit Heritage Dictionary
\cite{huet-2026-heritage}, keyed by MW headword with entry anchors; used as the
independent second reference.

\textbf{C15: the crosswalk and the sense map.} The DCS$\leftrightarrow$CDSL headword
crosswalk (98{,}606 rows) links corpus lemmas to dictionary entries and defines the
frozen universe's membership. A companion committed map resolves DCS word-sense synset
ids onto MW sense divisions for the WSD gold; its exact/overlap rows cover 32{,}369
(synset, lemma) pairs.

\textbf{DCS 2026.} The Digital Corpus of Sanskrit \cite{hellwig-dcs}: 5.69M tokens,
754{,}726 sentences, with per-token word-sense annotations (\texttt{m\_wordsem}) over
531{,}747 tokens, and a lemma inventory with IAST lemmas and grammar tags.

\textbf{E26: corpus frequency.} Lemma frequency sidecar (83{,}277 DCS lemmas) used for
the frozen sample's frequency strata.

Licenses: MW gloss text is public domain; DCS text and annotations are CC BY with
attribution to Oliver Hellwig; the frozen samples and gold tables are released under the
kosha public-tier license with those attributions. No external data were added.

\section{Evaluation Design}

\subsection{Frozen headword universe}

The two tracks share one frozen 500-headword sample (seed 730, deterministic builder,
SHA-256 digests of all inputs committed): the universe is the 11{,}316 SLP1 keys present
in A3 $\cap$ E26 $\cap$ C15, stratified 3$\times$3 by corpus frequency rank (high/mid/low)
and MW top-level sense count (mono / 2--4 / $\geq$5), 56 cells targeted, final $n=500$
(one cell exhausts at 52; every skip is logged). A candidate requires at least three
usable DCS attestation sentences (4--40 tokens, $\leq$2 per work). The freeze is the
committed sample plus attestation files; scoring never re-derives the join.

\subsection{Track 1: definition generation}

Each arm produces an English gloss for a headword given its grammar tag and up to five
DCS attestation sentences (context arms), or the headword and tag alone (the no-context
arm):

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{A0 (floor):} a seeded derangement of the gold glosses---metric floor and
judge-discrimination control.
\item \textbf{A1 / A2:} \texttt{deepseek-chat} \cite{deepseek-2025} with and without
attestations (temperature 0). The A1$-$A2 delta isolates what corpus context adds over
parametric memory.
\item \textbf{A3:} \texttt{deepseek-reasoner} with attestations.
\item \textbf{F1 (reference):} a stronger non-DeepSeek frontier model, same input
contract, generated in-session under a gold-free inputs projection; treated as a
reference point, not a reproducible baseline arm.
\end{itemize}

Scoring combines deterministic metrics (corpus and sentence BLEU and chrF via
sacrebleu \cite{post-2018-statistical}; bag-of-tokens F1) with a blinded LLM judge
(\texttt{deepseek-chat}, adequacy 0--5 vs.\ gold, blind to arm) admitted only under
gates: the judge must separate the A0 floor and correlate positively (Spearman
$\rho$) with chrF within system arms. Because MW 1899 is certainly in every candidate
model's pretraining data, the track measures \emph{reproduction and grounding}, not
generation from corpus evidence alone; the A1$-$A2 contrast and this framing carry the
caveat explicitly.

The second-reference study re-scores all five arms on the 333-headword MW$\cap$Heritage
subset against Huet's French glosses with a French-prompted blinded judge, asking
whether the arm ranking survives a change of reference dictionary and language.

\subsection{Track 2: gloss-grounded WSD with a deterministic gold}

\paragraph{Item and gold.} An item is one DCS sentence $\times$ one lemma of the frozen
universe, held out by a pinned deterministic fold: $\mathtt{crc32(sent\_id)} \bmod 5
= 0$ ($\approx$20\%). The gold sense is obtained with no manual step: the sentence's
\texttt{m\_wordsem} synset id and the lemma, joined through the committed WordSem$\to$MW
sense map, yield an MW sense \texttt{lemma\#ord}. The candidate inventory is the
lemma's attested
MW senses from the corpus-derived sense-frequency table. Items enter the pool only if
the lemma has 2--15 attested senses and the gold lies inside the inventory; monosemous
items are excluded by design. The pool funnel over the frozen universe: 531{,}747
tagged tokens $\rightarrow$ 26{,}570 gold-mapped $\rightarrow$ 5{,}115 held-out items
$\rightarrow$ 4{,}100 eligible polysemous $\rightarrow$ 300 sampled (seed 5877; 82
lemmas; inventory size $k$: min 2, median 3, max 12). In only 33.7\% of items is the
gold sense also the inventory's rank-1 sense, so the sample is not trivially MFS.

\paragraph{Arms.} \textbf{LLM:} \texttt{deepseek-chat} (temperature 0) receives the
headword with grammar, the IAST context sentence, and the numbered sense glosses, and
returns a JSON sense index. \textbf{MFS:} the training-fold majority sense per lemma
(the corpus-frequency prior the gold annotations themselves support). \textbf{Floor:}
uniform $1/k$. All arms are scored on identical items; the LLM and MFS comparison is
paired and evaluated with the exact McNemar test.

\paragraph{Fold integrity.} An earlier iteration of the gold spine keyed its train/test
split on Python's per-process-randomised \texttt{hash()}; its published split is stable
in expectation but not reproducible byte-for-byte. This track pins the split
(\texttt{crc32}) and re-derives its baseline under the pinned fold; we report the
discrepancy openly rather than silently redefining prior artifacts (Section
\ref{sec:fold}).

\section{Results}

\subsection{Track 1: definition generation}

All 500 items were generated and judged in every arm with zero empty candidates.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{lccccc}
\hline
Arm & BLEU & chrF & token-F1 & judge 0--5 & $\rho$(judge,chrF) \\
\hline
A0 floor & 3.13 & 12.02 & 0.097 & 0.19 & 0.117 \\
A1 chat + ctx & 3.05 & 19.57 & 0.297 & 4.32 & 0.391 \\
A2 chat no-ctx & 3.43 & 17.30 & 0.294 & 4.08 & 0.466 \\
A3 reasoner + ctx & 0.50 & 13.70 & 0.256 & 4.21 & 0.423 \\
F1 frontier + ctx & \textbf{5.14} & \textbf{24.35} & \textbf{0.340} & \textbf{4.60} & 0.415 \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\caption{Definition generation, 500-headword frozen sample. Judge gates pass in every
arm: the floor is cleanly separated and judge--chrF correlation is positive.}
\label{tab:defgen}
\end{table}

Three findings. \textbf{(1) BLEU is uninformative here}: the register-matched floor
matches the systems (3.13 vs 3.05), while chrF and token-F1 separate them cleanly.
\textbf{(2) Context adds little over memory}: A1$-$A2 $= +2.3$ chrF, $+0.003$ F1,
$+0.24$ judge---attestations sharpen sense selection, not content, consistent with MW
being in pretraining data. \textbf{(3) Metric choice changes the ranking}: the terse
reasoner is last on chrF (recall against 41.5-word golds) yet mid-pack under the blinded
judge. The frontier reference F1 leads every metric simultaneously (Table
\ref{tab:defgen}), with judge--chrF agreement inside the healthy band---the
surface/judge ranking concord the DeepSeek arms lack.

The polysemy penalty replicates in every frequency band (polysemy $\geq$5 is the worst
cell everywhere), and the frequency gradient is inverted---low-frequency cells score
\emph{higher}---because high-frequency MW entries carry long, structurally complex gold
glosses: a stratification effect, not model skill at rare words.

\paragraph{Second reference (Heritage, French).} On the 333-headword MW$\cap$Heritage
subset the arm ranking is \textbf{reference-invariant}: F1 $>$ A1 $>$ A3 $>$ A2 $>$ A0
under both the English and the French-prompted judge. The MW-familiarity premium---the
judge's excess kindness to glosses close to MW's own wording---is real but small
($+0.13$ to $+0.25$ on 0--5; bootstrap CIs exclude zero for every system arm; largest
for the memorisation arm A2, smallest for F1). Cross-lingual surface metrics are
near-degenerate; the judged score is the measurement there.

\hline
LLM (chat, gloss-grounded) & 0.543 & 300 \\
MFS (corpus prior) & \textbf{0.843} & 300 \\

\hline
\end{tabular}
\caption{Gloss-grounded WSD on held-out WordSem gold (300 polysemous items, pinned
fold). Paired LLM vs MFS: 10 vs 100 discordant pairs, exact McNemar $p<0.0001$.}
\label{tab:wsd}
\end{table}

The result is a clean negative (Table \ref{tab:wsd}): the gloss-grounded LLM arm scores
54.3\% against the MFS baseline's 84.3\% on identical items---30 points below, and only
22 points above the uniform floor. Zero API failures; all 300 picks parsed.

The gap is concentrated, not uniform. By inventory size: at $k=2$ the LLM reaches 0.700
(MFS 0.940); at $k\geq6$ it collapses to 0.183 (MFS 0.676). MFS degrades gracefully
with $k$; prompted gloss-matching does not. Three error classes dominate manual
inspection of wrong items: \emph{fine-granularity near-misses} inside one gloss family
(\emph{pit\d{r}}-: ``father and mother (du.)'' vs ``the Fathers, ancestors'');
\emph{literal-vs-metaphorical} splits (\emph{phala}-: ``fruit'' vs ``fruit =
retribution''); and \emph{cross-POS homonymy} (\emph{mad}-: pronoun base vs ``to
gladden'').

\subsection{Fold integrity}
\label{sec:fold}

The pinned-fold MFS baseline (0.843, polysemous-only, this sample) sits where the
earlier unpinned pipeline's published number lands (0.840 over all mapped test tokens,
monosemous included): the randomised split moved the estimate by less than a point but
made byte-level reproduction impossible. We therefore pin the fold, re-derive the
baseline, and flag the earlier artifacts as computed-under-one-process-seed rather than
rewriting them.

\section{Discussion}

\avnote{Interpretation slot --- the author's reading of the negative result goes here;
the lane offers the material reading only. The gold's corpus grounding means MFS
exploits precisely the usage prior that the annotation encodes, while a single-sentence
gloss match cannot; the supervised-encoder path of Hellwig et al.\ remains the
strong way to consume definitions for WSD, and a frequency-aware or hybrid
prompt is the obvious next ablation. Whether the paper leads with the resource
(reference-invariant eval + deterministic gold) or with the negative methodological
finding is an author's decision.}

The two tracks read together as a statement about where the difficulty of Sanskrit
computational lexicography lives. Definition content is largely recoverable from
parametric memory (Track 1, A1$\approx$A2), reference dictionary choice barely moves a
system ranking, and the real discriminative work happens at sense granularity---exactly
where Track 2 shows prompted models failing against corpus priors. For builders of
Sanskrit dictionary services the practical implication is that sense-level services
should lean on corpus-frequency priors first and treat gloss matching as a refinement
signal, not the reverse.

\section{Limitations}

\textbf{Judge human subsample.} The blinded judge passes its gates and agrees with
surface metrics on the frontier arm, but no human-scored subsample exists yet; a
human-scored subset is scheduled before camera-ready and no judge-only claim stands
alone until then. \textbf{Contamination.} MW 1899 is in pretraining data for every arm;
Track 1 claims are framed as reproduction+grounding throughout. \textbf{Single scoring
sample.} Track 2 reports one 300-item sample of a 4{,}100-item eligible pool; all
artifacts to scale it are committed, and the pinned fold makes any rescaling
reproducible. \textbf{Sandhi.} Context sentences are given in sandhied IAST as DCS
stores them; unsandhied variants exist in the dump and their effect is unmeasured here.
\textbf{Coverage.} The gold map covers exact/overlap crosswalk pairs; unresolved pairs
are excluded from scoring, and the funnel counts above make the exclusion visible.
\textbf{F1 arm.} The frontier reference arm is in-session and not temperature-pinned;
it is labelled a reference, not a baseline.
